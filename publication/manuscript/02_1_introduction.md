## 1. Introduction

Revenue-cycle management converts healthcare services into claims, payments, contractual adjustments, patient balances, denials, appeals, and accounts-receivable activity. The operational systems supporting that work produce large volumes of administrative data, but the ability to export data does not guarantee the ability to make reliable decisions from it. In many organizations, the practical analytics process begins with a user downloading reports, renaming files, moving them between folders, applying spreadsheet formulas, correcting labels, and combining outputs through local knowledge. The resulting report may be visually polished, yet the path from source to conclusion can remain difficult to reproduce, audit, or explain.

This problem is not limited to accuracy in the narrow sense. Data consumers judge quality through multiple dimensions, including relevance, completeness, consistency, timeliness, interpretability, and accessibility (Wang & Strong, 1996). Health-data quality frameworks similarly emphasize that fitness for secondary use requires systematic evaluation rather than an assumption that data extracted from an operational system are inherently trustworthy (Weiskopf & Weng, 2013; Kahn et al., 2016). In RCM, this distinction is commercially significant. A payer label that is split across several spelling variants may distort payer-level performance. A changed column name can silently null a measure. A duplicate export can double charges or payments. A missing denial mapping can suppress a root-cause trend. A claim-level file combined with a procedure-level file without an explicit grain can multiply financial values. These are not cosmetic defects; they can alter workqueue priorities, staffing decisions, payer escalation, and management interpretation.

Traditional reporting projects often place quality controls late in the process. Data are loaded, transformed, and visualized, after which anomalies are investigated when totals appear implausible. A governance-first model reverses this sequence. It requires every source file to be identified and dispositioned; every accepted report family to satisfy a versioned contract; every clean record to retain its source lineage; every classification to resolve through an approved mapping; and every financial transformation to satisfy defined invariants before the semantic layer is released. Under this model, a dashboard is the final consumer of governed data, not the place where data problems are hidden.

This paper addresses the following research question:

> **How can healthcare RCM organizations transform inconsistent operational exports into trustworthy analytics without manually altering source files or concealing unresolved data-quality issues?**

The paper makes four contributions. First, it defines a layered clean-room architecture designed specifically for fragmented RCM exports rather than for a single standardized clinical model. Second, it introduces explicit file-disposition and semantic-publication controls that prevent silent loss, duplication, drift, and unresolved classification. Third, it provides a deterministic status model in which GREEN, YELLOW, and RED represent operationally meaningful publication decisions. Fourth, it supplies a reproducible, entirely synthetic reference implementation, SQL schema, test suite, control matrix, and dataset that can be inspected without exposing protected health information (PHI), client data, or proprietary source extracts.

The term *clean room* is used here in an engineering and governance sense: a controlled environment in which source evidence is preserved, transformations are separated from raw inputs, mappings are explicit, quality findings are recorded, and only validated outputs are promoted. It does not imply that the system itself satisfies every legal, regulatory, contractual, or security requirement for handling production healthcare data. The public artifact deliberately contains no real patient-level or client-level information.
